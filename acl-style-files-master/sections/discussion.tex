\paragraph{Methodological Implications}
Traditional approaches to studying moral evolution---game theory, agent-based models, population genetics---abstract away cognitive processes, encoding behaviour as fixed strategies. Our LLM-based paradigm relaxes these assumptions: agents perceive, remember, reason, and decide through cognitively realistic architectures. This yields two advantages. First, cognitive factors such as moral type observability and communication bandwidth become first-class experimental variables. Second, we obtain action-level resolution---tracing \emph{why} a type fails by examining individual agents' reasoning at specific steps (supplement \S\ref{sec:case_studies}), not merely observing aggregate outcomes.

\paragraph{Insights into Moral Evolution and Modern Societies}
Across our experiments, morality is consistently favoured by selection while selfishness is strongly disfavoured---selfish agents tend to decline first in most settings, though they occasionally persist in a small number of runs (Table~\ref{tab:replicate_outcomes_main}). Among moral types, kin-focused morality dominates the Baseline (6/8 runs), benefiting from a compact, low-coordination cooperation unit---the family---that avoids the overhead of broader alliances. Yet even there, universal and reciprocal agents together survive in 5 of 8 runs, showing that broader moral circles remain competitive. In the remaining settings, broader moral dispositions fare better overall: reciprocal morality dominates under Resource Scarcity (3/4 runs) through coordinated coalition hunting, while universal morality shows the most consistent viability across challenging conditions (8/12 runs), though it is not always the single dominant type. When pressures intensify, the small family unit can no longer sustain itself, and broader cooperation becomes more advantageous.

These patterns resonate with real human societies: purely selfish individuals are rare across cultures; many people are strongly family-oriented, analogous to kin-focused agents; and a substantial portion extends moral concern to broader communities. This coexistence may reflect a stable evolutionary outcome in which different moral strategies persist under different environmental regimes. The prevalence of kinship-focused cooperation in prehistoric societies \citep{holden2003matriliny, mattison2011evolutionary} may reflect conditions analogous to our Baseline, while the subsequent expansion of moral circles \citep{singer1981expanding} may have been driven by increasing environmental pressures that made small-group strategies insufficient.

\paragraph{Insights for Moral Theory}
Our findings support the expanding circle model \citep{singer1981expanding} as a unifying moral framework. Empirical results confirm that broader, yet self-consistent moral circles generally produce superior evolutionary outcomes. The model integrates diverse moral characteristics---care, fairness, reciprocity, loyalty---while naturally incorporating kin selection and reciprocal altruism. Our work further highlights how cognitive factors---particularly the reliability of in-group identification---critically affect moral evolution, potentially explaining the emergence of norms that facilitate group recognition.

\paragraph{Our Contribution and Scope}
The primary objective of this paper is to introduce this novel framework and demonstrate its versatility for investigating a wide range of questions in moral evolution. It is a complementary approach to traditional research in anthropology and evolutionary biology. Its strength lies in providing rich, detailed simulations and enabling the study of complex factor interactions. 
Consequently, we do not aim to provide an exhaustive set of experiments to definitively resolve any single factor. Instead, our goal is to provide a robust tool for future studies to explore these questions in greater depth.

\paragraph{Conclusions}
We present an LLM-based simulation framework for systematically studying complex social evolutionary dynamics, exemplified by moral evolution in prehistoric hunter-gatherer contexts. Our experiments show that morality is consistently favoured while selfishness is strongly disfavoured, with kin-focused morality dominating under abundant resources and broader moral circles prevailing under environmental pressure. By incorporating cognitive realism, our approach yields mechanistic insights inaccessible to traditional game-theoretic frameworks, establishing a novel paradigm for social evolutionary research.
